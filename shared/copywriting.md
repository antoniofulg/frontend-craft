# Marketing copy

Read when writing, editing or refreshing persuasive text: page copy, headlines,
calls to action, scripts and captions. This guide stands alone; it does not
depend on any one surface. Product labels, errors and operational feedback are
not marketing copy; use the consuming skill's guidance for interface text.

## Commercial brief and claim inventory

Before writing new copy, recover the audience's situation, desired outcome,
current alternative, decision stage and material objections from supplied context.
Distinguish user, buyer and approver when they need different evidence. Ask only
for missing facts that would otherwise force an invented offer or claim.

Maintain a small working inventory for claims used in the copy. Return it in the
response for planning or review; update an existing brief only when the requested
work authorizes editing it. Use this shape rather than a second marketing ledger:

| Claim | Evidence and scope | Qualification | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proposed promise, number, testimonial or guarantee | Supplied source, date/population and permission where relevant | Limits needed for an accurate reading | Supported, needs confirmation, or omit |

A design example is not evidence for a customer count. Distinguish an aspiration
from a product capability already available. Keep the promise, supporting proof,
CTA destination and post-click experience aligned; an honest narrower promise
is preferable to an unsupported stronger one. Flag contradictions with approved
copy rather than silently changing commercial facts.

## Write, edit and refresh marketing copy

Establish editing scope from the request and supplied text: polishing wording,
refreshing facts or rewriting the argument. A wording edit preserves the intended
message, offer, qualifications and next action. A rewrite can change the argument
within the requested scope; flag a needed positioning or offer decision rather
than silently making it. For new copy or changed claims, use the
[commercial brief and claim inventory](#commercial-brief-and-claim-inventory).
Reuse established context; ask only for missing facts that block accurate copy.

### Diagnose the situation first

Decide which situation the request is, and say so in one line with the evidence
from the input, because the diagnosis sets how much may change:

- **Blank or generic:** a one-line ask, or company-speak with no point of view.
  Build the argument from the brief and product context. There is latitude on
  angle, never on facts: no invented statistics, customers or results.
- **Real voice present:** a draft with an angle, opinion or recognizable voice,
  even if rough. Restore it. Keep the author's argument and vocabulary and fix
  the buried lead, vague lines and structure.
- **Point edit:** "fix this headline", "tighten this paragraph". Change only what
  was asked plus the minimum needed to make it fit, and return the rest untouched.

In the last two, read judgment out of the input. Vocabulary is a voice choice:
"ship" and "stuff" do not become "deploy" and "solutions". Where the author spent
words shows priority, what is missing shows scope, and the angle is the
positioning: deepen it rather than substitute a preferred one. Given a writing
sample, match its sentence length, punctuation habits and a few words the default
voice would not choose. Before returning, give each change a reason; a change
that is merely different, not better, is reverted.

### Generate headline alternatives

When asked to explore a headline, treat the families below as a menu for
generating alternatives, not as templates to fill. Pick the family that matches
the audience's awareness, draft two or three candidates on that one axis, and
discard a shape that only works once its slots are filled with invented
specifics. Every slot comes from the claim inventory.

| Lead with | Shapes to try | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | "{outcome} without {pain}"; "{outcome} by {mechanism}"; "turn {input} into {outcome}"; "{outcome} in {timeframe}" | The outcome and any timeframe are supported and scoped |
| Problem | "Never {unpleasant event} again"; a question that names the pain; "stop {pain}, start {gain}" | An absolute promise ("never") survives the stated limits |
| Audience | "{product type} for {audience}"; "you don't need {skill or resource} to {outcome}" | The audience is who actually buys and can use it |
| Differentiation | "the {category} that {differentiator}"; "the {opposite of the usual process} way to {outcome}" | The differentiator is verifiable and a competitor could not claim it unchanged |
| Proof | "{number} {people} use {product} to {outcome}"; the key benefit stated plainly | The figure is real, current and cleared for use |

Try the name swap on each candidate: if it stays equally true with a competitor's
name, look for a more specific fact in the supplied evidence, or flag that the
differentiator is missing rather than inventing one. State each option's audience
fit or tradeoff. A formula, a single "without" or a "never again" headline is
ordinary and acceptable once; the formula does not replace a supported claim.

### Decline claims with an unlock path

When a requested claim is not supported by the source material, do not write it
and do not drop it silently. Name the claim, say why it falls short, offer the
closest supportable alternative and state the evidence that would unlock the
original: a review export, a written guarantee policy, a certification, a citable
study or dataset. A declined claim with an unlock path is an action item for its
owner; without one it is a dead end. Mark the gap in the copy with a visible
placeholder, such as `[NEED: proof]`, instead of replacing a vague phrase with a
plausible number.

### Voice and naturalness

Use the existing brand guidance and approved writing samples to identify voice:
formality, vocabulary, rhythm and acceptable humor. Keep the voice recognizable
while adapting tone to the audience and situation. Product names, domain terms
and legal meaning remain stable. Neutral technical or legal prose can be the
appropriate voice; do not inject personality merely to make it sound human.

Replace inflated praise, vague authority and formulaic filler with the supported
mechanism, example or outcome. Changing synonyms alone does not make a vague
claim useful. Keep author notes, editing commentary and chatbot offers out of
publishable copy. Preserve factual scope and meaningful uncertainty; never add
personal experience, customer quotations or numbers for a more natural voice.
Treat stylistic patterns as editing cues, not evidence of who wrote the text;
punctuation bans and fixed sentence or paragraph counts are not quality measures.

### Review signals in generated-sounding copy

These shapes often appear when copy is written on autopilot. Each is a cue to ask
whether the sentence carries a supported claim, not a ban, a cap or evidence of
authorship. All are ordinary rhetoric that can be right when chosen for a reason:
a single "no card required" beside a CTA, a reader's own question, three features
when there are three, a fragment that lands a real payoff. Judge the sentence
against its facts, not against a count.

| Signal | Ask | When it fails, restore the fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contrast reveal: "not X, but Y", "this isn't X, it's Y" | Did anyone hold belief X? Is Y supported? | State Y with its reason or cost; correct a real misconception with the evidence |
| Negation list: "no X, no Y, no Z" | Is each absence real and a reason to buy? | Say what happens, in order; state a real absence once, near the action |
| Trailing pile-on: a complete claim, then added clauses | Does each clause add a new fact? | End at the claim; give a worthwhile benefit its own concrete sentence |
| Question then answer: "The result? Faster." | Does the question label the writer's own claim? | Keep the answer |
| Colon reveal: "The best part: it learns." | Is this a drumroll for an ordinary statement? | Write the plain sentence; keep colons for lists and labels |
| Reflexive threes: "fast, secure and scalable" | Do the facts supply three, or did rhythm? | Use the items the facts support; the strongest concrete claim often replaces the list |
| Uniform rhythm: equal sentence and section length | Does length follow the idea or a pattern? | Let a section run as long as it has something to say, and shrink or cut the rest |
| Abstract subject: "the platform empowers teams to..." | Who does what? | Make the customer or the product the actor with a verb: "your team ships without a ticket" |
| False range: "from startups to enterprises" | Do the ends bound a real span? | Name the segment served or the steps covered |

When several signals cluster in one paragraph, rewrite it from the facts rather
than patching each; synonym swaps produce new habits. Vary the fixes, since a
single repair repeated everywhere, such as ", because..." after every reveal,
becomes a new pattern. Keep the brand's register: a formal sentence that makes a
specific claim is fine, and flattening it into clipped startup copy is its own
tell. Most lines should be plain and declarative; a page needs only a few
punchy moments, because lines that all try to land cancel each other. Do not
invent a statistic, anecdote or quotation to make a vague line specific; use the
decline-with-unlock route.

### Refresh factual content

Check affected capabilities, prices, terms, examples, statistics and links against
current product behavior and dated evidence. Retain accurate historical dates and
qualifications; changing a date alone does not refresh the underlying evidence.
When the core promise still holds, update the affected details. A changed
audience, offer or argument can justify a rewrite within the authorized scope.
Name unresolved facts instead of filling gaps with plausible claims. Content age
or declining traffic alone does not establish that the wording caused a problem.

### Recheck the edited copy

Select checks for the actual change: clarity, voice, claim support and consistency
between the promise, CTA and destination. Read changed lines in their surrounding
context so a smoother sentence does not lose a limit or contradict nearby text.
For implemented text changes, verify affected wrapping and controls through the
consuming skill's interface checks, and recheck any public-page contracts the
change touches (URLs, metadata, analytics, forms) with that skill's redesign
guidance. Return usable copy and material unresolved facts; explain consequential
choices without annotating every line or requiring a fixed sequence of editorial
passes. Editorial review does not prove higher conversion.
