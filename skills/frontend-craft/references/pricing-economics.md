# Pricing economics and free access

Read when proposing prices, increases, freemium or willingness-to-pay research.
Apply [commercial strategy](commercial-strategy.md), including source terms;
use [monetization](monetization.md) for the offer's interface and trial funnel.
Treat calculations as scenarios until the product supplies their inputs.

## Establish economic and customer-value bounds

Estimate service cost per account and period: infrastructure, third-party tools,
AI usage, support and other variable delivery costs. Include usage distribution
and expensive segments; an average alone can hide unprofitable heavy users.
For cost `C` and target gross-margin fraction `g`, required revenue for that margin
is `C / (1 - g)`, using a consistently defined cost basis. This is a target-margin
floor, not a complete profitability model; acquisition and fixed costs remain.

Estimate the buyer's alternative: current spend, labor, missed business or risk.
Separate revenue recovered from profit recovered, time saved from cash actually
saved, and demonstrated outcomes from projections. The resulting value estimate
is a reference for research, not proof of willingness to pay or a universal price
ceiling. Competition, credibility, switching effort and budget constrain capture.

A low price may signal weak value or attract a different segment, but price alone
does not identify poor customers. Compare fit, support cost and retention by cohort.
When price-only differentiation is weak, investigate underserved jobs and complaints
about existing alternatives to find a specific value advantage.

## Evaluate free access as an acquisition investment

Compare freemium, paid entry and trials using the same acquisition budget or
eligible visitor base and observation window. Show every denominator: visitors,
signups, activation, paying accounts and retained contribution. A signup-rate win
can lose once nonpayer costs, conversion delay and churn are included.

Estimate free-user stock over time, usage limits, token or compute costs, storage,
support, abuse and the funding horizon. Measure whether referrals, collaboration
or organic distribution from free users offset that cost; do not assume virality.
Short time-to-value and low service cost can make free access attractive. Expensive
usage or slow activation may favor a capped demo, usage trial or paid start.
A free plan can still be strategic when its explicit budget and benefits justify it.

Include acquisition payback and sensitivity to conversion, usage and retention.
For stable monthly contribution, a rough payback is acquisition cost per paying
account divided by monthly contribution per payer; label this an approximation.
For changing margins, delayed payment or high churn, use cohort cash flows instead.
Keep currencies, periods and the treatment of free-user costs consistent; count
those costs once. Read [retention](retention.md#connect-retention-to-acquisition-payback)
when departures occur before payback.

Payback units follow the contribution interval. If using annual revenue per
account `A` and gross-margin fraction `g`, the constant-contribution approximation
in months is `12 × CAC / (A × g)`. A zero or negative contribution has no finite
payback under that approximation. This is not observed cash recovery; use the
actual cohort cash flows when timing or retention makes the approximation unsuitable.

## Research and change prices deliberately

Segment interviews by job, buying authority, alternatives and budget. Probe recent
purchases and tradeoffs, present concrete packages and prices, and distinguish
stated willingness from actual purchasing behavior. Use paid pilots or observed
new-customer offers where authorized; hypothetical survey answers are uncertain.

Consider price review when value, costs or the served segment materially change.
Explain what improved and why the offer fits. A new-customer pilot can preserve
existing contracts while providing evidence; record channel, season and cohort
differences before attributing results to price. Use randomized tests only when
volume and commercial constraints support a credible comparison.

Evaluate purchase incidence, revenue and contribution per eligible account,
plan mix, sales-cycle length, support, refunds and later retention. A higher price
can offset fewer purchases; it can also increase delivery expectations and churn.
Report sensitivity and an observation window, not a guaranteed profit lift.
Any change to existing prices, entitlements or billing needs that implementation
scope and approved customer terms; a page audit remains a recommendation.
