# Product strategy, feature discipline and differentiation

Read when deciding what to build, simplify, surface or retire; diagnosing poor
feature adoption; or changing the audience or differentiation of a product.
A small interface repair does not require a roadmap audit. Use the relevant
section and stop once the requested decision is supported.

Apply the commercial brief, evidence boundaries and upstream source terms in
[commercial strategy](commercial-strategy.md). This reference adapts the
upstream product-strategy and AI-era-differentiation modules; the linked source
record owns attribution. Product claims and projected gains remain hypotheses
until supported by evidence from this product.

## Match the intervention to the product's stage

Establish which uncertainty the requested work should resolve. Before traction,
verify the audience, costly problem and willingness to pay; compare another
feature with a sales conversation or distribution experiment. Express the
feature's cost as delivery, support and maintenance effort and, when financial
data exists, its effect on runway. A faster implementation does not itself
resolve an untested offer or activation problem.

For an established product, inspect adoption, successful task completion and
retention before recommending expansion. Improving the core flow, reliability
or attention hierarchy may deliver more value than adding advanced controls.
Use [positioning and distribution](positioning-distribution.md) when audience,
market viability or acquisition motion is the unresolved decision.

A deliberate move upmarket changes buying roles, service expectations, market
size and willingness to pay. Recheck those assumptions and align the offer,
copy, demonstrations and visual confidence with them. Explain that tradeoff;
more power-user functionality is not automatically maturity or a better fit
for the current audience.

## Decide whether a feature deserves its ongoing cost

For the features relevant to the decision, collect the intended user/task,
reach among eligible users, appropriate use frequency, outcome evidence,
sales contribution, dependencies and maintenance/support burden. Classify:

| Role | Evidence to seek | Candidate decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Enables the main job or recurring value for the target audience | Improve reliability, accessibility and prominence |
| Strategic | Supports a material buying requirement, infrequent essential task or differentiated segment | Preserve with an appropriate place in the journey |
| Unproven or redundant | Weak fit or duplicated value, with little outcome evidence | Investigate discoverability, consolidate, defer or propose retirement |

Low usage alone does not justify removal: annual reporting, recovery,
accessibility and contractual requirements can be essential. Distinguish an
unneeded capability from one users cannot find or understand. Before a proposed
retirement, identify affected users, data, integrations, contracts and migration
or communication needs. A review proposes the decision; it does not authorize
removing capabilities or changing commercial promises.

For new work, apply the upstream **Swiss Knife filter** as questions: does it
serve the chosen audience, reinforce the core promise, justify cognitive load,
and earn its operational cost? Then compare the smallest validating version
with deferral, using expected reach, revenue or user benefit, confidence and
effort. Use consistent scoring units if a score helps compare candidates;
adding raw user counts, money and subjective impact produces a meaningless
number. Mandatory repairs and contractual obligations retain their own priority.

Record cancellation-driven requests with customer, date, context and repeated
demand. Check fit, severity and revenue exposure before reshaping the roadmap.
Communicate the actual commitment or uncertainty; an angry cancellation neither
proves demand across the audience nor makes the request irrelevant.

## Diagnose feature fatigue without a universal cutoff

The upstream **Swiss Knife Index (SKI)** divides features meeting a chosen usage
threshold by all features in the inventory. Its example uses adoption by more
than 40% of active users in 30 days and flags a ratio below 0.3. Treat these as
the author's illustrative settings, not a validated benchmark or deletion rule.
Define what counts as a feature, an active/eligible user and the observation
window before calculating; segment roles and plans with different entitlements.
For infrequent tasks, choose a window that contains a real opportunity to use.

Use the index only as a prompt to investigate complexity alongside task success,
discovery, recurring use, support and commercial contribution. It can hide the
value of specialist workflows or reward arbitrary inventory granularity.
Feature-fatigue research does not validate this particular index or its cutoffs.
A product can look attractive during feature comparison yet be hard to learn
in practice; observe both buying comprehension and real task completion.

Prefer progressive disclosure for useful specialist capabilities: keep them
reachable at the relevant task or advanced setting, with search/navigation that
still works. Ask what can be consolidated or moved out of the primary path
before adding another prominent control. There is no universal ideal feature
count, adoption percentage or one-feature-in/one-feature-out requirement.

## Design discovery around need and measure repeated value

Trace the intended user's habitual path. Surface the capability where a task,
empty state or limitation makes its benefit relevant. Explain the result and
next action; use behavior-triggered guidance when a signal of need exists.
A changelog, email or “new” badge can announce a feature but does not establish
that users understand when to use it.

Make the value-driving action prominent in the relevant context, accounting for
role, frequency and urgency. Existing habits impose a learning cost: explain
why switching helps and preserve familiar successful interactions where possible.
Use [activation](activation.md) for the first useful result and
[commercial visuals](commercial-visuals.md) for attention and presentation.

Measure eligible-user discovery → successful first use → repeated use at a
meaningful frequency → user/commercial outcome. Choose the review threshold
from task cadence and baseline; low repeat use may reflect poor fit, timing or
experience. Distinguish correlation with retention from a causal improvement.

## Differentiate through useful experience and credible identity

When competitors share similar technology, identify the specific workflow the
product handles better: domain language, relevant defaults, integrations,
collaboration and fewer steps to a useful result. A niche label without matching
behavior does not establish differentiation. Preserve focus on the core promise
when considering broader audiences or an “all-in-one” offer.

Express identity consistently in typography, imagery, tone and interaction,
using the existing design direction or an explicitly scoped evolution. Show
actual product outcomes and recognizable workflows; pair promises with credible
evidence. A polished surface may signal care but cannot establish security,
reliability or commercial uplift. Use [landing pages](landing-pages.md) for the
hero and proof, and [commercial visuals](commercial-visuals.md) for composition.

Build durable preference through familiar efficient workflows, useful history,
integrations and collaboration when they serve the task. Evaluate expansion
from shared value, not forced invites or blocked exit. Use [retention](retention.md)
when proposing changes to accumulated value or switching experience.

Spend saved implementation effort on helpful error recovery, prevention of
predictable mistakes, clear confirmations and an onboarding path beyond an empty
dashboard. Respect attention with a focused next action and relevant detail;
do not infer a user's cognitive ability or addiction from generic claims about
short-form video. In design-to-code workflows, identify the maintained source of
truth and synchronization contract; reuse real components and inspect drift
rather than rejecting or trusting an entire tool category by default.

## Finish with a bounded decision

For each recommendation, state the affected audience/task, observed barrier,
proposed change, evidence and validation. Identify assumptions about strategic
fit or economics. Reuse the [review format](review.md#commercial-findings);
a feature inventory or new strategy document is needed only when the task
requires it.
