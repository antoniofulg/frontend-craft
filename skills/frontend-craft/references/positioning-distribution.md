# Positioning, market fit and distribution

Read when selecting an audience, diagnosing acquisition quality, choosing a
self-serve or assisted journey, evaluating a launch/channel/referral plan, or
checking whether revenue targets fit the reachable market. For an ordinary page
review, use existing positioning and inspect only the entry context needed for
that page; a market-sizing exercise is not a prerequisite.

Apply the brief, evidence boundaries and upstream source terms in
[commercial strategy](commercial-strategy.md). This reference adapts the upstream
positioning-icp-and-gtm module and related product-strategy principles; the linked
source record owns attribution.

## Establish audience and evidence of demand

Recover the target buyer/user, triggering problem, cost of leaving it unsolved,
current alternative, ability to pay, access channel and founder/team access to
the audience. Distinguish a deliberately underserved segment from everyone who
could theoretically use the product. Check that the reachable segment can
support the delivery and support model; specificity is useful when it improves
fit and reach, not simply because the niche is small.

When validating an offer, seek real commitments appropriate to the product:
paid prototypes, pilots, purchases or transparent pre-sales with clear delivery
terms. Interview interested people who decline about the obstacle to paying now.
Separate curiosity, signup, willingness to pay, actual payment and retained use.
Early customer counts in the source are examples, not gates requiring ten or
twenty customers before design or launch. Provide enough usability and credibility
to test the offer fairly; pilot commitments must not promise unavailable work.

For claims of product-market fit, examine paid value, repeated use, retention,
unsolicited recommendation and acquisition economics across relevant cohorts.
The source's sequence from audience/pain through validation to scale is a map of
dependencies, not a universal certification process. Investigate the missing
evidence rather than declaring fit from a launch spike or a fixed customer count.

## Size the opportunity from explicit assumptions

Use market sizing when the decision needs it:

- **TAM:** the customers or spending addressable by the defined category/offer.
- **SAM:** the portion serviceable given geography, capabilities, price and
  delivery constraints.
- **SOM:** the portion plausibly obtainable within a stated horizon, supported
  by channel reach, conversion, retention, competition and operating capacity.

Record units, sources, date, overlap between segments and price assumptions.
Prefer a bottom-up customer count and realistic annual spend when available.
SOM is not an arbitrary percentage of SAM; population alone is rarely a useful
paid-product market. Revisit the definitions when the offer or audience changes.
Avoid importing the source's market percentages, timelines or investor thresholds
as requirements. Use ranges where evidence is incomplete and identify the
assumption most likely to change the decision.

## Match the buying motion to real friction

Assess time and work to first value, implementation/data migration, purchase
risk, approvals, price relative to the buyer's budget, support economics and
whether collaboration or sharing naturally brings users. Choose the journey:

| Motion | Conditions to investigate | Interface consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-serve | Users can evaluate, start and get value with sustainable support | Useful trial/demo, clear price and direct path to activation |
| Sales-assisted | Evaluation or commitment benefits from explanation, migration or procurement help | Qualified demo/contact path, implementation evidence and clear next steps |
| Hybrid | Segments differ or assistance becomes useful at a later stage | Self-serve entry with contextual human help or enterprise evaluation |

A price ceiling, ten-minute activation target, huge TAM or native virality is
not a universal definition of product-led growth. Treat the source's four
conditions as diagnostic dimensions; test whether the product actually drives
acquisition, activation and expansion with viable economics. A failed trial can
reflect missing value, poor fit or onboarding as well as a need for sales help.

## Locate the growth bottleneck before prescribing a redesign

Trace channel and audience → message → landing page → action → first useful
result → payment → retained value. Low qualified traffic directs investigation
toward reach, channel, demand and positioning. Traffic without payment requires
checking fit, offer, price, trust, task failure and product value. Neither pattern
alone proves that distribution or design is the sole cause.

Segment by entry source and awareness: someone responding to a concrete problem
may need a different explanation and next step from a branded search visitor.
Keep the ad/referral promise, page and delivered experience consistent. Use
[landing pages](landing-pages.md) or [activation](activation.md) for a diagnosed
journey barrier; building more features may postpone the missing sales evidence.

## Work backward from a revenue target

Use one period, cohort and denominator consistently. For a simple subscription
scenario, target recurring revenue divided by assumed revenue per paying customer
estimates required customers. Gross additions must cover both desired net growth
and lost customers. Convert those additions backward through measured or explicitly
assumed visit-to-paid and impression-to-visit rates to estimate traffic and spend.
With multiple plans, expansion or contraction, model those flows separately.

Check channel capacity, acquisition cost, gross contribution margin, support
capacity and cash timing. A constant-churn lifetime approximation assumes stable
hazards; use cohort retention when available. State uncertainty and run plausible
scenarios rather than importing the source's conversion, click-through or churn
percentages. Higher signup conversion can still produce worse economics when
qualification or retention deteriorates. Use [experimentation](experimentation.md)
when selecting metrics and validation.

## Focus channel experiments and referral design

Use the source's Bullseye approach as a prioritization aid: list plausible
channels, run a manageable set of small tests, then focus resources where evidence
supports qualified acquisition and retention. Rank candidates by expected impact,
evidence/confidence and cost or ease using consistent scales. Scores express
judgment; they are not forecasts. Choose concurrent tests from budget and ability
to attribute results, not fixed ring sizes. Record audience, promise, effort,
measurement window and the condition for continuing or stopping each test.

Fix material landing/activation failures alongside distribution work before
scaling spend. Compare channel cohorts through paid or retained value; cheap
clicks alone do not identify a good channel.

For referrals, first establish a useful experience people have reason to share.
Offer the invitation after an actual result or at a naturally collaborative
moment. Test recipient-benefit framing (“give a teammate extra capacity”) against
other accurate presentations. Choose cash or product benefits from audience,
cost, abuse risk and product fit; the source's preference for noncash gifts is
a candidate, not a universal rule. Explain both parties' benefits, eligibility,
limits and material incentives. Make invitations optional and user-directed.
Measure qualified referred activation and retained value net of rewards, fraud
and support cost. A referral program amplifies demand; it does not establish
that the underlying offer delivers value.

## Deliver the requested decision

Return the audience, diagnosed bottleneck, proposed page/journey or experiment,
evidence and validation, with material economic assumptions visible. Use the
existing [commercial review format](review.md#commercial-findings) where applicable.
A strategy review proposes channels and offers; it does not itself authorize
ad spend, outreach, price changes or changes to customer commitments.
